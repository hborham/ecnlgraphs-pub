How to read a BeatGraph

A short tutorial for ECNL Graphs. No math required. The demo below runs the real ranking steps on six made-up teams — the same layout engine and the same method as the live graphs.

What you're looking at

Each graph shows one competition — one league, or one tournament. Every team is a badge. The best team sits at the top, and arrows point from a winner down to the teams it beat. Read an arrow as a sentence: A beat B. The graph is a picture of the season so far: who beat whom, all at once.

There is one graph per competition. Teams only appear with the opponents they actually played, so each graph stands on its own.

Three words to know

BeatWin. A direct win. When A beats B, A earns a BeatWin over B, weighted by the goal difference — winning 4–0 counts for more than winning 1–0. Draws create no BeatWin: nobody beat anybody. (Demo step 2.)

BeatPath. A chain of BeatWins. If A beat B and B beat C, then A has a BeatPath to C, even if A and C never played each other. The whole ranking is built out of these chains. (Demo step 3.)

BeatLoop. A win cycle: A beat B, B beat C, and C beat A. Nobody is clearly on top, so the loop has to be resolved before anyone can be ranked. (Demo steps 4–5.)

How a loop gets resolved

In the demo, the results are: Aces beat Blaze by 2, Blaze beat Comets by 2, and Comets beat Aces by 1. That last result is the weakest link in the cycle, so its weight (1) is subtracted from every link in the loop:

LinkBeforeAfter
Aces beat Blaze21
Blaze beat Comets21
Comets beat Aces1gone

The weakest result drops out, the other two survive with reduced weight, and the cycle is broken: Aces sits above Blaze, Blaze above Comets. The smallest loops are resolved first, and as little data as possible is removed. On the interactive views, the Broken Loops button shows you exactly which links were pruned.

Why some wins have no arrow

Only the smallest set of arrows is drawn. If A beat B and B beat C, the fact that A sits above C is already implied — drawing a third arrow would add clutter without adding information. So an arrow is left out whenever a BeatPath through other teams already says the same thing. The ratings still count every game; only the drawing is simplified. On one published example, 285 games reduced to 38 arrows. In the demo above, every arrow earns its place — none is implied by the others.

On the interactive views, tapping a team highlights its BeatPaths and reveals its direct wins, including ones the drawing left out.

What the number means

Each team has a rating, roughly on a scale from −10 to 10. Higher means a stronger record against the teams in that competition: the rating counts every BeatPath in and out, weighted by goal difference, so beating strong teams matters more than beating weak ones. (Demo step 6.)

Two cautions. The rating describes what has already happened — it does not predict the next game. And ratings only compare teams inside the same competition: a 6.5 in one league and a 6.5 in another were earned against different opponents, so they are not directly comparable.

Gold rings, empty graphs, and scores

Gold rings mark bracket teams, and only appear on tournament graphs (Surf Cup). In a league every team is in the competition, so rings would be meaningless and are left off.

Empty graphs mean the schedule is posted but no games have been scored yet. Those graphs fill in on their own as results come in.

Scores sit under every graph on the Graphs view. On the swimlane and canvas views, the Scores tab lists every game in the league (filterable by team), and tapping a team shows its own scores table.

The three views

Swimlane — the default view: the interactive D3 graph, teams in rank layers, tap a team for its details. Canvas — the interactive Cytoscape view: the same graph on a canvas you can pan and zoom. Graphs — the graph as a picture, with the full scores table as readable text, at the Graphs view. All three show the same data and the same filters. The swimlane and canvas views link to each other with your selection preserved, and the Graphs view links to both.

Use the five filters — Season, Gender, Age, Type, Competition — to pick a competition. The page address holds your selection, so you can copy the link to share exactly what you're looking at.

Where it comes from

The game data comes from the league schedules (Total Global Sports) and the Surf Cup results site, and the graphs are rebuilt weekly. The ranking method is the Weighted Method from BeatGraph. The interactive views are built with D3, dagre, and Cytoscape.js.
